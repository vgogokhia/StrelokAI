Coriolis effect in shooting: does Earth's rotation matter?

The Coriolis effect is a small shift in the bullet's impact caused by the Earth rotating during its flight. In the northern hemisphere the bullet drifts right; shooting east it hits slightly high and shooting west slightly low. For a .308 at 1000 yards at 40° latitude it is about 3 inches sideways and up to 4 inches vertically.

Horizontal: always right in the north

While the bullet flies, the ground under it keeps turning. From the shooter's point of view the bullet seems to curve: to the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern. The effect depends on latitude (zero at the equator, largest at the poles) and time of flight, and it does not depend on which direction you shoot.

Vertical: the Eötvös effect

Shooting east, the bullet moves with the Earth's rotation, and the surface curves away beneath it: it hits slightly high. Shooting west, it hits slightly low. North or south, the vertical part is zero. For our .308 example at 40° latitude and 1000 yards:

Does it matter?

Inside 600 yards, no: it is smaller than normal wind uncertainty. At 1000 yards and beyond, a few inches can decide a hit on a small target, and extreme long range (ELR) shooters always include it. Horizontal Coriolis by latitude

At the equator the horizontal part vanishes, and it grows with latitude. The vertical Eötvös part is largest at the equator and zero at the poles.

FAQ

Which way does Coriolis push a bullet?

To the right in the northern hemisphere and to the left in the southern hemisphere, regardless of the direction you shoot.

How much is Coriolis at 1000 yards?

A few inches: about 3 inches horizontally for a .308 at 40° latitude, plus up to 4 inches high or low when shooting east or west.

Do snipers account for Coriolis?

At extreme ranges, yes. Military and competition ballistic solvers include it, but at typical engagement distances it is much smaller than wind error.

Does Coriolis matter for .22 LR?

No. At rimfire distances the time of flight is short enough that Coriolis is a small fraction of an inch.
