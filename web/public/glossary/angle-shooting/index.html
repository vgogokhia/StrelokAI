Uphill and downhill shooting: the rifleman's rule

When you shoot uphill or downhill, gravity acts only on the horizontal part of the distance, so the bullet drops less than on flat ground and you hit high in both directions. The rifleman's rule: use the come-up for the horizontal distance, which is the line-of-sight range × cos(angle).

Updated September 30, 2026 · ballistics.ge

The rifleman's rule

horizontal distance = line-of-sight range × cos(angle)

At 30°, cos is 0.87, so a 600-yard target up a slope needs about the come-up for 520 yards. At 10° the cosine is 0.98 and the effect is small. Many laser rangefinders give you this "angle-compensated range" directly.

Worked example: 30° uphill at 600 yards

Dialing the flat-ground come-up here would put the shot about 14 inches high, a miss on most targets. The cosine rule is a good approximation up to moderate angles and ranges; a calculator that solves the angled trajectory is more exact at long range and steep angles.

Measuring the angle

Use a rangefinder with an inclinometer, an angle cosine indicator on the scope, or your phone. Our calculator can read the shot angle from the phone's sensors: lay the phone along the rifle and tap to capture. Uphill and downhill angles give nearly the same correction; downhill is not "the opposite" of uphill.

Common mistakes

  • Using the angle-compensated range for wind. Wind drift depends on time of flight, which follows the line-of-sight range. Use the real range for wind and the horizontal range only for elevation, or let the calculator do both.
  • Thinking downhill needs more come-up. Both directions hit high with flat-ground dope.
  • Ignoring small angles at long range. At 1000 yards even 10° changes the come-up by a noticeable fraction of a MIL.
  • Guessing the angle. Slopes look steeper than they are. Measure the angle.

FAQ

Do you aim high or low when shooting downhill?

Low, or dial less come-up. Both uphill and downhill shots hit high if you use the flat-ground come-up.

At what angle does it start to matter?

Past about 10–15° at medium range. At 300 yards and 10°, the difference is usually less than an inch; at 600 yards and 30° it is more than a foot.

Does angle change wind drift?

Very slightly, because the time of flight is almost the same. Use your normal wind hold for the line-of-sight range.

What is the improved rifleman’s rule?

It applies the cosine to the drop rather than the range. It is slightly more accurate at long distances, but a calculator that solves the angled trajectory directly is the most accurate option.
